Regarding the first objective, Sipowa et al. proposed a
distributed OD framework for satellite swarms that combines
Covariance Intersection (CI) with Unscented Information
Filter (UIF) [2]. By using CI, each agent only needs to
estimate and store their own state estimates and covariances,
which makes the framework scalable. However, their work
targeted relative OD, and assumed small separations between
satellites, restricting it from being used for satellite constellations which typically have larger satellite separations. They
also did not model nor estimate onboard clock offsets and
drifts in their work, which needs to be corrected for satellite
constellation operations.

Abstract—Future deep space exploration, particularly the upcoming lunar missions, stands to benefit significantly from networks of small satellites, given their cost-effective communication and monitoring capabilities. However, challenges like
frequent network partitions, high communication delays, and
the demand for onboard orbit determination (OD) and time
synchronization present obstacles for these satellite networks.
One of the key problems in this context is how to schedule
satellite communications and generate a contact plan. This
paper presents an integrative solution that combines navigation
and communications performance for contact plan optimization. Additionally, we propose a novel two-way inter-satellite
link (ISL) scheme to acquire range and differential clock bias
estimates that take into account rapid inter-satellite range shifts
and signal propagation delays. Building on these, a distributed
orbit and clock bias estimation algorithm is introduced, which
emphasizes the use of batch processing to improve orbit observability. Performance metrics are validated through a simulation
of a lunar navigation constellation, assessing OD error, time synchronization error, and inter-satellite communication delay. The
combined insights and proposals presented, especially in terms
of navigation and communications performance tradeoffs, offer
promising avenues to enhance the operability and efficiency of
small satellite networks.

Regarding the second objective, Yan et al. proposed a
contact plan optimization algorithm that accommodates the
requirements of both OD and communication [3]. They
formulated and solved a constraint optimization problem that
sets inter-satellite communication delay as the optimization
objective while enforcing the satellites to switch ISL pairs at
every timeslot for improved geometrical diversity of the ISL
directions. They also developed a heuristics algorithm based
on simulated annealing to solve the problem. However, the
performance of the optimized plan was evaluated only for the
communication delay, and not for OD or clock synchronization errors.

1. I NTRODUCTION

The goal of this paper is to extend and fuse these two lines
of work to efficiently coordinate long-range ISLs within
a small satellite constellation to meet both navigation and
communication requirements. First, we optimize the contact
plan solving for the Pareto front of ISL geometrical diversity
and communication delay by incorporating the result of a
covariance analysis simulation directly in the objective function. Second, we propose a two-way ISL scheme to obtain
the range and differential clock bias estimates, considering
rapid changes in the inter-satellite range and propagation
delays. Finally, we propose a distributed orbit and clock bias
estimation algorithm, which processes the two-way range
and differential clock bias measurements in batch. The
proposed framework can estimate absolute orbits instead of
relative orbits, thanks to the improved observability of the
proposed batch processing scheme. Finally, integrating the
three segments together, we demonstrate the performance of
the optimized contact plan and the distributed state estimation
algorithm by evaluating the OD error, time synchronization
error, and inter-satellite communication delay in a simulated
lunar constellation.

Networks of small satellites will play a key role in future deep space exploration, supporting orbit and surface
missions thanks to their cost-effective communication and
monitoring capabilities. One representative example is
NASA’s Lunar Communications Relay and Navigation Systems (LCRNS) [1], a network of satellites providing networking and navigation services in the lunar region.
These satellite networks, however, are subject to frequent
partitions and high delays due to the limitation in the resources of small satellite platforms to establish multiple intersatellite links (ISLs), long distances between satellites, and
signal blockage by planetary bodies. At the same time, future
satellite networks in deep space will be desired to perform
onboard orbit determination (OD) and time synchronization,
with the limited capacity of the Deep Space Network. To fully
exploit the potential of small satellite networks, onboard OD
and timing algorithms are desired to be scalable and flexible
to dynamic network topology. Therefore, the implementation
of ISLs within the satellite network needs to be designed
considering the two objectives: 1) facilitating distributed
onboard OD and time synchronization via inter-satellite ranging, and 2) enabling data exchange among satellites with low
communication delay.

Consensus and Diffusion Kalman Filters
The distributed estimation problem has been investigated
extensively for the past two decades. Earlier work by Spyer et
al. [4] proposed a decentralized Kalman Filter, in which local
Kalman filters communicate with all other nodes to estimate
the state of fully connected networks. Later, Olfati-Saber
et al. [5] proposed a distributed Kalman Filter, where each
agent only communicates with its neighbor to reach a consensus on estimates with other nodes on the network. While
these consensus-based methods have excellent mathematical
properties, they impose significant timing constraints on the
network, requiring the network to run the consensus step at
much higher rates than the filter updates [6]. To overcome this
challenge, Cattivelli, et al. [7] proposed a diffusion Kalman
filter, in which each node performs a single-step update using
a convex combination of neighbor node estimates. However,
these distributed filtering approaches assume that the states to
be estimated among the agents are the same. This assumption
does not apply to our scenario, where the primary interest of
each satellite is in estimating its own state.

Various multi-agent frameworks have been proposed for
collaborative orbit determination of networked spacecraft
swarms with different sensor suites. Example frameworks
include DiGITAL [11] system for nanosatellites with multiGNSS receivers, Angles-only Absolute and Relative Trajectory Measurement System (ARTMS) [12] for angles-only

number of states and N is the number of satellites in the
swarm. While this formulation enables accurate handling of
the correlations between state estimates of each satellite in
the swarm, they are not scalable to large swarm sizes, as the
computational cost of the Kalman Filter grows square with
respect to the state size.

The satellites will use ISL for both navigation and communication. In this section, we define the satellite states to be

One caveat of the above works, which simulates absolute
orbit determination with inter-satellite range measurement,
is that they do not model the two-way raging procedure in
detail and assume that instantaneous range, clock bias difference, (and range-rate) measurement could be obtained within
certain errors without delay. In reality, errors arising from
light time delays, clock drifts, and changes in the crosslink
range during signal propagation cannot be ignored, especially
in large separation scenarios.

We use TDMA to coordinate the communication process
between satellites. Each transmitter-receiver pair will be
assigned a time slot to communicate with the other satellite
based on the contact plan. The detailed mathematical formulation of the measurement equations is derived in Section 6.
We have two types of satellites: the anchor satellites (nodes)
and the non-anchor satellites. The anchor nodes has access to
an external navigation source (e.g. GPS, ground station links)
that can be used to continuously update its orbit and clock
bias estimates. Other satellites (e.g. non-anchor nodes) rely
on ISL range and relative timing measurement to estimate
their orbit and clock bias. The goal of each agent is to obtain
a sequential state estimate for their own state xi , without
estimating the states of other spacecraft in the swarm xj . This
is achieved by the combination of an EKF and CI, as it will
be explained in Section 6.

Topology Handling Based on FSA
The constellation network topology changes periodically due
to the movement of the satellites and can be modeled as an
FSA [26]. The FSA states are derived by dividing the system
period of the satellites T into N equal-length intervals, disregarding those satellites that are only partially visible within
a given state. In other words, two satellites are said to be
visible from each other in a state if they are within lineof-sight throughout the entire state duration. Therefore, the
contact plan design can be performed in each FSA state based
on fixed topology.
Satellites require frequent communication to increase the
precision of autonomous navigation and typically have fewer
ISLs than the number of visible satellites. Therefore, they
need to switch their ISLs to enable inter-satellite ranging with
different satellites. As in previous work [24], we further
divide one FSA state into M equal-length time intervals
denoted as contact plan periods.

8. C ONCLUSION
Designing a contact plan for navigation satellite networks necessitates consideration of both inter-satellite ranging, which
measures the distances between satellites for accurate positioning, and inter-satellite communication, enabling data
exchange between satellites to form a global network. In
this context, we target the complex problem of scheduling
crosslinks within networks of small satellites in lunar orbit.
Similarly to previous work on Earth navigation satellite
constellations, we frame the contact plan design problem
as a constrained multi-objective optimization problem, considering both navigation and communications performance.
However, we consider satellites with limited resources that
need to rely on anchor nodes, i.e., a group of a few satellites
with better navigation capabilities. Given the problem’s complexity, we resort to a heuristic algorithm based on simulated
annealing, a stochastic optimization method that gradually
reduces the search space and decreases the likelihood of
accepting suboptimal solutions. Simulation results show that
our proposed method optimizes contact plans with reduced
network delay and improved orbit determination accuracy,
according to design criteria. Different solutions, however,
present tradeoffs in terms of communications and navigation
