Abstract—Low Earth Orbit (LEO) satellite constellations are
poised to become key enablers of global connectivity. However,
their dynamic topologies, high latency, and intermittent links pose
significant challenges for traditional IP routing protocols. This
paper evaluates the applicability of OSPFv3 in LEO networks using a novel emulation framework that integrates accurate orbital
modeling with full-stack network simulation. Our framework
decouples topology generation from network emulation, enabling
protocol-independent testing in realistic conditions. We analyze
OSPFv3 performance using the Iridium constellation as a case
study, assessing protocol convergence, stability, and routing efficiency under worst-case scenarios. Results reveal the limitations
of default OSPFv3 configurations in LEO environments and offer
insights into potential optimizations for future integration of
satellite networks into global IP infrastructures.

I. I NTRODUCTION
Low Earth Orbit (LEO) satellite networks are satellite
systems that operate at altitudes between 500 and 2,000
kilometers above Earth. Unlike traditional geostationary satellites, LEO satellites orbit the planet more quickly and in
greater numbers, enabling low-latency high-speed communication. These networks are increasingly used to provide global
broadband coverage, especially in remote or underserved areas, and play a growing role in modern telecommunications
infrastructure.
LEO satellite networks are composed of M satellites uniformly distributed across N orbital planes, typically with an
inclination below 90◦ . Each satellite is a cube or a modular
unit built from 10 × 10 × 10 cm cubes, with antennas on five
faces—including the one oriented toward Earth’s center. The
Earth-facing antenna provides ground-satellite links (GSLs),
while two lateral faces establish intra-plane inter-satellite links
(ISLs) with neighboring satellites in the same orbit. These

II. R ELATED W ORK
The design of routing algorithms in Low Earth Orbit (LEO)
satellite networks remains a critical research area due to
their highly dynamic topology. Frequent link interruptions and
topological changes, especially in inter-satellite links (ISLs),
introduce significant challenges to the scalability, stability, and
efficiency of conventional routing protocols. These difficulties
are reflected in the ongoing standardization efforts led by
organizations such as 3GPP and the IETF to adapt routing
strategies for LEO satellite environments [17].
Researchers have proposed various strategies to address
these issues, including adapting distributed protocols originally
developed for terrestrial networks and employing centralized
approaches, often based on Software-Defined Networking
(SDN). Distributed methods offer autonomous management
of topological dynamics and benefit from native IP compatibility, facilitating seamless integration with existing ground
networks. In contrast, centralized strategies divide the network
operation into discrete snapshots where topology is considered
static during each interval. Routes are precomputed offline
and sent in advance to the network nodes, which apply them
synchronously. While effective in predictable environments,

centralized schemes may exhibit reduced resilience to unexpected link failures [17].
Previous evaluations of distributed protocols have discarded
the applicability of OLSR (Optimized Link State Routing)
in satellite environments. Although designed for ad-hoc networks, OLSR is unsuitable for low node counts and fast link
changes, and exhibits limited efficiency when using mechanisms such as multipoint relaying [8]. Similarly, reactive
protocols such as AODV have shown limited viability in
broadband satellite connectivity, particularly for large-scale
LEO constellations. Existing ad hoc approaches that depend
on distance vector routing or flooding are generally inadequate
for highly mobile environments [11].
Among the distributed protocols studied, OSPF has received significant attention. However, the literature presents
contradictory findings regarding its effectiveness. Some studies
suggest that minor enhancements are sufficient to adapt OSPF
to LEO networks, while others argue that a comprehensive
redesign is necessary. Most works proposing modifications
to OSPF evaluate their enhancements against the baseline
protocol using default terrestrial configurations. This often
leads to misleading conclusions, as inappropriate simulations,
flawed emulation environments, inadequate parameter tuning,
or unsuitable chosen evaluation metrics may fail to accurately
reflect the true behavior of LEO networks.
In response to this fragmented research landscape, the
present study aims to conduct a systematic and thorough
evaluation of OSPFv3 in LEO constellations. The objective
is to identify the operational issues and challenges of the
protocol and propose adaptations that enable its efficient use in
future space-ground integrated infrastructures, where control
plane stability is vital for next-generation services. One of the
few comprehensive studies in this area is by Wang et al. [8],
which highlights the lack of a reliable reference for OSPF
performance in satellite systems. Their results indicate that
OSPF suffers from significant packet loss and routing overhead
in large-scale LEO deployments, making it unsuitable without
substantial adaptation. Other comparative studies, such as that
by Yan et al. [14], contrast OSPF with the Named-Data LinkState Routing protocol, a link-state algorithm for InformationCentric Networking (ICN). Their findings suggest that OSPF
outperforms its counterpart in terms of convergence speed and
control overhead.
Several enhancements to OSPF have been proposed to
improve its scalability in LEO networks. For instance, Zhang
et al. [6] introduce Area-based Satellite Routing, which partitions the constellation into stable inter-area segments to
reduce routing updates. Li et al. [15] define clusters within
four quadrants and adopt a joint intra- and inter-area routing
scheme based on periodic link-state updates.
The hybrid approach, which combines distributed protocols
with centralized precomputed routes, has also gained attraction. Fischer et al. [5] were the first to propose Predictable
Link-State Routing, which leverages the predictability of satellite motion. Based on this approach, Yan et al. [19] developed
SATNET-OSPF, an extension of OSPF that uses cross-layer

Geolocation-based forwarding strategies offer another line
of research. Li et al. [12] present SER, a routing algorithm
that computes shortest paths using only orbit and satellite
IDs, thereby avoiding link-state flooding and reducing intermediate node overhead. Wang et al. [20] introduced MPLF,
a distributed, convergence-free architecture that incorporates
satellite orientation and location into next-hop decisions. Yang
et al. [16] proposed IFAR, which combines geolocation-aware
forwarding with linear programming to match satellites with
ground stations while taking into account capacity constraints.
Similarly, OPSPF [9] calculates routing tables based on satellite positions in real time and activates on-demand routing only
upon detection of unexpected topological changes.
Our work makes a substantial contribution to the reassessment of the OSPFv3 protocol in a realistic LEO satellite environment. By implementing an innovative emulation
framework that combines accurate orbital modeling with
comprehensive network simulation, this study establishes a
foundational knowledge base and provides valuable insights
for the future design of routing protocols in such dynamic
systems. The evaluation results demonstrate that OSPFv3 is
capable of maintaining robust connectivity and achieving low
convergence latency, even under continuous satellite mobility.
These findings align with and support the recent guidelines
outlined in RFC 9717 [21], which presents a scalable routing
architecture for satellite networks based on existing IETF link
state routing protocols, enhanced by awareness of scheduled
link connectivity changes. This RFC recommends IS-IS as
the preferred protocol due to its inherent flexibility in area
definition.
To address predictable topological variations, RFC 9717
advocates the proactive assignment of high metrics to links
expected to disconnect. This approach enables traffic redirection toward alternative routes before link degradation occurs,
facilitating a more secure and coordinated transition compared
to abrupt link removal. By avoiding problems such as incomplete state propagation and timing inconsistencies, the strategy
ensures network convergence ahead of the actual topological
event. This proactive metric assignment mechanism is one of
the main conclusions confirmed by our findings.

A. Dynamic Topology Modeling
This subsection presents the module in charge of generating
the dynamic topology of the satellite network. It relies on
a modified version of the Hypatia orbital propagator [10] to
compute satellite positions, active inter-satellite links (ISLs),
and visibility with ground stations over time. The original
satgenpy module was extended to calculate ISL and Ground
Station Link (GSL) parameters, determine satellite–ground station visibility, and generate a sequence of GML files describing
the network’s temporal evolution.
In the orbital propagator, three types of input data are used
in the emulator: the configuration of the satellite constellation
and its active inter-satellite links, and the set of satellites
within communication range of each ground station along
with their respective distances. Both of these are provided for
each time interval to capture the temporal evolution of the
network topology. The third input is the geographical location
of the ground stations, which remains static. This information
is processed to generate a single JSON file per snapshot,
describing the resulting LEO network topology.
Each generated JSON file represents the network topology
at a given snapshot through two main components. One
component defines a set of labeled virtual networks, each
corresponding to an inter-satellite or ground-to-satellite link,
using default configuration parameters. The other component
describes all nodes in the network, including satellites and
ground stations, each identified by a label derived from the
input data. For each node, a set of configuration parameters
is provided, including those related to the routing protocol,
which in this case is OSPFv3.
The generation of each snapshot involves three main stages.
First, the spatial configuration of the constellation is extracted,
including satellite positions and ISLs with their associated
delays. Then, based on the relative positions of satellites and
ground stations, the most suitable connections are established
using a hard handover criterion. Finally, all this information is
combined into a structured JSON description that captures the
network state at that instant, serving as the basis for subsequent
emulation stages.

updates to their latency. For ground stations, events reflect the
changing visibility of satellites as they move in and out of
range. Each event is scheduled to occur at a specific moment
during the emulation, and includes the relevant parameters for
applying the changes dynamically.
The output of this emulation process consists of detailed
packet-level traces. These include routing protocol messages,
which allow for the analysis of routing dynamics over time.
Additionally, performance metrics such as packet loss, delay,

A. OSPFv3
OSPFv3 [3], the version of OSPF designed for IPv6, was
published in 1999 and updated in 2008, as specified in RFC
5340. It retains the core mechanisms of OSPFv2, such as
the flooding algorithm for information distribution and the
use of Dijkstra’s shortest path algorithm for route calculation.
However, OSPFv3 introduces important changes to support
IPv6, including modifications to address formats and router
identifiers, and the removal of built-in authentication due to
IPv6 security features. The protocol organizes routers into
areas, but in highly dynamic networks like LEO satellite
constellations, the concept of stable areas is challenging to
apply.

Routers in OSPFv3 exchange five types of messages directly
over the IP layer to build and synchronize their link-state
databases, ensuring all routers within the same area have
a consistent view of the network topology. Hello messages
maintain neighbor relationships, while Link State Advertisements (LSAs) carry detailed network information using a
reliable flooding mechanism. This synchronization is essential
to compute loop-free, optimal routes. Of the several LSA types
defined, this work focuses on Router LSAs, Link LSAs, and
Intra-Area Prefix LSAs, which respectively describe router
interfaces, neighbor link-local addresses, and IPv6 prefixes
within an area.
Despite its strengths, OSPFv3 faces scalability challenges
in large satellite networks. The volume of LSAs generated
can be very high, increasing control message overhead. Additionally, the computational effort for route calculation grows
exponentially with the number of nodes, limiting the protocol’s
responsiveness to frequent topology changes typical in LEO
satellite networks.
The OSPFv3 configuration for satellites includes assigning
an IPv4 address from the 10.0.0.X/32 range to the loopback
interface, which serves as the router identifier in OSPFv3, and
an IPv6 address from the 2002::X/128 range, where X is an
8-bit identifier for the satellite. For ground stations, the IPv4
address is assigned from the 10.X.255.255/32 range, while all
ground stations share the IPv6 anycast address 2002::ffff/128.
Anycast is used to enable routing toward any available ground
station, with the protocol automatically selecting the nearest
one. Interfaces connected to ISLs and GSLs are configured as
point-to-point links, which prevents the election of designated
routers and avoids the generation of Network LSAs. Additionally, the Hello and Dead intervals are reduced to one and two
seconds respectively, allowing for rapid neighbor discovery
and failure detection in OSPFv3.

V. C ONCLUSIONS AND F UTURE W ORK
This work presents a comprehensive study on the applicability of OSPFv3 in LEO satellite networks, supported by a
custom emulation framework that combines realistic orbital
dynamics with full-featured network stack simulation. Our
results demonstrate that although OSPFv3 can maintain connectivity under dynamic LEO conditions, its default behavior
and timing parameters are not well suited for highly timevariant topologies.
The proposed framework represents a significant advancement in the evaluation of routing protocols for satellite communications. By decoupling the topology generation from
protocol operation and emulating realistic ground and intersatellite interactions, it enables precise analysis of protocol
performance in complex scenarios. This contribution lays the
groundwork for future developments in adaptive routing strategies and highlights the necessity of protocol enhancements to
support next-generation space-based Internet services.
Building upon the satellite network emulation framework
developed in this study, future work will focus on the evaluation of additional routing algorithms. In particular, we plan
to assess not only traditional approaches but also routing
strategies specifically designed to exploit the predictable temporal evolution of satellite network topologies. Given the
deterministic nature of orbital mechanics, such time-aware
algorithms may offer significant improvements in performance
and reliability compared to static or purely reactive solutions.
