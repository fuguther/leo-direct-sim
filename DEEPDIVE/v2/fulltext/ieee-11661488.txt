Abstract—Low Earth orbit (LEO) satellite networks exhibit
rapidly changing topology and time-varying traffic hotspots,
which makes hop-by-hop routing highly sensitive to local congestion and state staleness. Existing routing methods either rely
on global path computation or use plain local observations, while
graph-enhanced approaches often focus on generic neighborhood
representation rather than direct comparison among candidate
next hops. To address this issue, this paper proposes a Local Graph-Aware Routing method (LGAR) for dynamic LEO
satellite networks. LGAR organizes the current node, reachable
candidate neighbors, and candidate links into a local graph,
and then constructs structured action representations through
node encoding, relation message extraction, and attention-based
context aggregation. The resulting representations are integrated
into an off-policy actor-critic framework to support adaptive hopby-hop routing decisions. Experiments under the hub-inversion
setting show that LGAR achieves an average total delay of
47.64 ms and an average queueing delay of 5.81 ms while
maintaining a delivery rate of 99.93%. Compared with MATMR,
LGAR-NoGraph, and GRLR, LGAR reduces the average total
delay by 12.38%, 12.85%, and 30.75%, respectively. Additional
scenario, ablation, and scalability results further show that
LGAR generalizes beyond the main setting and that its gain
mainly comes from local graph modeling and relation-aware
action encoding.
Index Terms—LEO satellite networks, hop-by-hop routing,
local graph representation, reinforcement learning, hotspot traffic

I. I NTRODUCTION
LEO satellite constellations are expected to provide lowlatency and wide-area connectivity, but routing in such networks remains challenging because the network state changes
continuously with satellite motion and uneven traffic demand
[1]
. In practical systems, hotspot traffic generated by gateway
concentration, regional service bursts, or migrating traffic
centers can easily create local queue buildup and delay amplification. As a result, the quality of a hop-by-hop routing
decision depends not only on geographic progress toward the

979-8-3195-0613-9/26/$31.00 ©2026 IEEE

destination but also on instantaneous neighborhood congestion
and link conditions around the current forwarding node.
Existing routing methods can be roughly divided into
three categories. Traditional shortest-path and global-pathbased methods are efficient when topology snapshots and
link costs are accurate, but their online adaptability is limited
because global state collection and path maintenance become
costly under dynamic traffic [2], [3] . Distributed learning-based
methods improve scalability and online decision making, yet
many of them still rely on plain vectorized local observations,
which makes it difficult to explicitly model the structural differences among candidate next hops. Graph-enhanced routing
methods further exploit neighborhood information, but they
often focus on generic graph representation learning or multiagent coordination rather than direct action-level comparison
among candidate forwarding directions [4]–[6] .
For hop-by-hop routing, the decision object at each step
is one next-hop action selected from a small candidate set
instead of an entire end-to-end path. Therefore, the routing state should be organized in a way that matches the
action granularity and the information actually available at
decision time. Compared with a complete global-topology
representation, a local graph centered on the current node
is more suitable for distributed online routing because it directly captures destination guidance, candidate links, and local
queueing conditions in the neighborhood where the decision
is executed. More importantly, the goal of this work is not
merely to use neighborhood information, but to build actionaligned local graph representations so that each candidate
forwarding direction can be evaluated under a shared local
topology context. This distinguishes LGAR from MATMRstyle vectorized local observations and from generic neighborhood graph encoders such as GRLR and GraphPR. This action
alignment distinguishes LGAR from generic neighborhood
encoders, because each local edge is explicitly treated as one
executable forwarding option rather than as an undifferentiated

Based on this observation, this paper proposes a Local
Graph-Aware Routing method, abbreviated as LGAR, for dynamic LEO satellite networks. The main contributions of this
paper are threefold. First, a local-graph-based state modeling
method is developed for hop-by-hop routing, where the current
node, candidate neighbors, and candidate links are organized
into an action-aligned local graph. Second, a local graph
encoding and structured action representation mechanism is
designed to improve discriminative comparison among candidate next hops through node encoding, relation message
extraction, and attention-based context aggregation. Third,
experiments under hub-inversion traffic as well as additional
traffic scenarios demonstrate that LGAR reduces end-to-end
delay while maintaining a high delivery rate, and the main gain
comes from queueing delay reduction. The remainder of this
paper presents the problem formulation, the LGAR method,
and the experimental validation.

A. Experimental Setup
Experiments are conducted in a Python/PyTorch-based LEO
network simulator under a Walker-Delta constellation with
altitude 600 km, inclination 53◦ , 18 orbital planes, 16 satellites
per plane, and phasing parameter 3. Three gateway regions,
namely Beijing, Luxembourg, and Dubai, are considered. The
main setting is a hub-inversion traffic scenario, where dominant gateway-pair demand is periodically reversed and creates
time-varying local congestion. The training and evaluation
durations are 60 s and 45 s, respectively. The base Poisson
traffic rate is 4.8 packets/ms, the normal-packet probability is
0.72, and the two packet sizes are 0.052 for normal packets
and 0.016 for small packets.
The proposed method is compared with SPF, GRLR,
MATMR, and GraphPR. SPF serves as a traditional globalpath heuristic baseline, MATMR represents a local-observation
cooperative baseline, and GRLR and GraphPR serve as
neighborhood-graph-enhanced learning baselines. All reported
evaluation results are averaged over three random seeds. The
main evaluation metrics are delivery rate, total delay, and
queueing delay. Additional experiments are further organized
into multi-scenario generalization, ablation analysis, and scaleextension tests. These added evaluations are intended to separately examine robustness across traffic patterns, the contribution of individual model components, and the practicality of
online deployment as the constellation grows.

Table 1 reports the averaged delay and delivery metrics
under hub-inversion evaluation. LGAR attains an average total
delay of 47.64 ms while maintaining a delivery rate of 99.93%.
Compared with MATMR, LGAR-NoGraph, and GRLR, the
average total delay is reduced by 12.38%, 12.85%, and
30.75%, respectively. In contrast, SPF delivers only 10.82%
of packets because shortest-path forwarding cannot react to
hotspot-induced queue buildup in time, and GraphPR suffers
from both high mean delay and a long tail. Relative to
MATMR, the non-substitutable gain of LGAR comes from
action-aligned local graph modeling instead of plain vectorized
local state, which cuts queueing delay from 10.78 ms to 5.81
ms.

Table 3 consolidates all LGAR variants into a single ablation comparison and isolates the contribution of different
model components. Removing local graph modeling causes the
largest performance drop, which verifies that action-aligned
local graph state construction is the primary source of improvement. Removing relation messages still increases the total delay from 47.64 ms to 52.84 ms, while removing attention
aggregation increases it to 50.98 ms. Therefore, both relationaware action encoding and attention-based context aggregation
contribute to the final gain, while the largest reduction in
queueing delay is obtained only when all components are
jointly retained.

D. Scalability and Deployment Cost
Table 4 summarizes the deployment-oriented statistics of the
learned routing policies. The reported route time is measured
on the CUDA-based simulator host as route time s/route calls
and is reported as mean ± std over profiling epochs 2–6
after excluding warm-up epoch 1. LGAR requires 1.11M inference parameters and about 4.23 MB storage, which remains
moderate compared with MATMR. Observation construction
and action masking remain below 0.10 and 0.01 ms/call,
respectively, across the profiled methods. Although MATMR
has a similar route-time mean, Table 1 shows that LGAR
achieves much lower queueing delay. Compared with GRLR
and GraphPR, the gain of LGAR is also not explained by using
a larger or slower online model; instead, the ablation results
indicate that the main advantage comes from relation-aware
action encoding on top of local graph state modeling.
Table 5 further examines whether the proposed method
remains effective when the constellation becomes larger. When
the constellation size increases from 288 to 864 satellites,
LGAR maintains a delivery rate around 99.94%, while the
total delay and queueing delay remain stable and even slightly
decrease. The average route time only changes from 0.24
ms/call to 0.26 ms/call, which suggests that, under a fixed
four-direction action space, the proposed local graph-aware
policy remains computationally efficient as the constellation
grows.
